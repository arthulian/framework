[A Model of Integrity]

I. Principles and Disciplines

1. Epistemic Integrity

Intellectual Honesty:
  • Clarify your opponent's argument in the most fair and coherent way understood.
  • Use the core criteria of that clarified argument as the gold standard to immanently critique your opponent's original argument.
Impartial Objectivity:
  • Apply the same empirical standards to all conclusions (the golden rule), ensuring every inference is substantiated by objective evidence rather than assumptions or imposed narratives.
Impartial Responsibility:
  • Using objective standards to audit your own motives, separate genuine care from ego-driven caretaking, and only assign accountability to outcomes that fall within your actual control.

2. Emotional Regulation

Ego Decoupling:
  • Treat your assumptions and their underlying mental/emotional states as data points that inform you about your current state, not as directives that define you
  • Set personal boundaries to create the space to choose rational responses instead of compulsive patterns. People-pleasing and controlling behaviors are examples of defensive compulsions triggered by fear and sustained by a lack of self-trust.
Acceptance and Recovery:
  • Accept that sometimes you need the support of others/society by setting aside your ego and dropping the pretense of invulnerability.
  • Treat planned recovery and rest as an essential maintenance for sustained performance.
Stress Responses:
  • Under overwhelming pressure, set healthy boundaries and disengage. Survival is not avoidance.
  • Under mild/moderate pressure, use controlled exposure to reframe difficulties/threats as manageable challenges to expand your tolerance window.

3. Self-Concept

Self-Worth:
  • Human dignity rests on the innate disposition and capacity to pursue noble aims through conscientious choice. It is measured by the quality of your thoughts and intentions.
  • Self-sufficiency, when purely defensive, is counterdependence dressed up as strength, born out of fear of being vulnerable or rejection.
  • Develop a growth mindset by seeing setbacks as skill-deficit feedback rather than as personal flaws or grounds for self-condemnation.
Self-Confidence Loop:
  • A. Internalize achievements produced by your deliberate efforts to establish confidence in your capabilities.
  • B. Measure confidence in proportion to your capability level.
  • C. Update your confidence whenever new evidence demonstrates a change in your capability.
  • D. Use your increased confidence to progressively tackle harder challenges.

4. Sustainable Action

Situational Analysis:
  • Identify how circumstances distort judgment; use counterfactual simulations to separate the objective facts from the biased presuppositions you stacked on top of these facts.
Strategic Focus:
  • During cognitive overload, shed non-essential tasks to refocus on basic needs and prevent future burnout.
  • Avoid the illusion of always being in control and recognize that some situations are contingent on external forces and unpredictability.
  • Don't allow sunk costs to influence future decisions.
Sustainable Perseverance:
  • Sustained persistence is tempered by fortitude and nourished by the absolution of self-acceptance, making it sustainable.
  • Once we define the underlying purpose (the why) of an action, we can set goals and objectives (the means) without becoming attached to the resulting outcomes.
  • Build resilience through gradual controlled exposure.
  • Maintain commitment to long-term purpose and values, while staying adaptable in strategy.

II. Four Epistemic Virtues that are embodied in this model

• Truthfulness: Conform beliefs, representations, and behavior to objective reality as best understood.
• Accountability: Own the consequences of your beliefs and conclusions proportionally to your knowledge, constraints, and intent, actively revising them when new evidence warrants.
• Coherence: Consistently align behavior with values, beliefs with evidence, and self-concept with empirical reality.
• Incorruptibility: Persevere in truth and sound judgment, actively withstanding the internal and external pressures that compromise integrity or moral character.
